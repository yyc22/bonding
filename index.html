Bonding theory and model limits

Electronegativity describes attraction for bonding electrons. Its difference is a guide to polarity, not a universal classifier. Ionic and covalent character form a continuum.

Covalent bonds

Covalent bonding involves shared electron density. Identical bonded atoms share equally; different atoms can share unequally. A difference below 0.4 is used here only as an approximate guide to weak polarity. There is no upper electronegativity cutoff that automatically makes a bond ionic.

Why H–F is polar covalent

HF contains a shared bonding electron pair drawn toward fluorine: Hδ+–Fδ−. This table uses H = 2.20 and F = 3.98, so ΔEN = 1.78. Some textbooks use rounded values and obtain 1.9. Neither result changes HF into an ionic compound. Hydrogen bonding between HF molecules is distinct from the covalent bond within each molecule.

Ionic and metallic bonding

Ionic bonding is attraction between oppositely charged ions, typically throughout a solid lattice. Electron transfer is a useful model of ion formation, not the definition of the bond. Metals and many alloys have delocalised electrons. Neither model can be assigned reliably from ΔEN alone.

The simulator identifies selected named ionic solids: NaCl, KF, MgO, CaF₂ and MnI₂. Other metal–nonmetal and metalloid combinations require a specific compound and structure. Selecting two elements does not prove that a stable molecule exists.

Bond polarity is not molecular polarity

Molecular shape determines whether bond dipoles cancel. CO₂ has polar bonds but is nonpolar overall; water's bent shape gives it a net dipole. Nonpolar bonding does not by itself imply low reactivity.
